AI scams in 2026: how to protect your small business from phishing and deepfakes

Oct 14, 2026 · 6 min read · Raccoon Now
Raccoon Now · Blog

Digital fraud no longer relies on typos and broken links. In 2026, anyone trying to scam a business can use generative AI to write flawless emails, clone a familiar voice in seconds, and build a fake website indistinguishable from the real one. For a small business without a dedicated security team, that changes the game: the signs that used to give a scam away — clumsy writing, a pixelated logo, an obviously fake domain — matter less and less. Here's how this fraud actually works today, and what you can do about it this week without a large-company budget.

1. AI-written phishing doesn't look like phishing anymore

A phishing email used to be recognizable by its clumsy wording or by asking for information in a generic way. Today, a language model can write that same email in the exact tone of a real vendor, mention your company by name, reference an invoice with a plausible number, and attach a PDF with the correct branding. Part of that personalization comes from public information: your company's website, LinkedIn, an Instagram profile. The more information about your business sits online, the more convincing a targeted attempt against your admin or purchasing team can be.

2. Voice deepfakes: the "urgent manager" scam

AI voice cloning no longer needs hours of sample audio — a few seconds from a public video are enough to produce a believable clip. The scheme security consultancies most frequently report in 2026 is a call or voice message that imitates a real executive, urgently requesting a wire transfer, a change of bank details, or the purchase of gift cards, always leaning on urgency and secrecy ("don't mention this yet"). The simplest defense is still the most effective one: any unusual payment request gets confirmed through a separate channel, by calling a number you already had saved — never the one included in the same message.

3. Why your small business is a target, not an exception

It's easy to assume these attacks only go after large corporations. In practice, several cybersecurity vendors have flagged the opposite trend: many attackers deliberately choose smaller companies with fewer formal defenses, because they're easier to compromise and can sometimes serve as a way in toward larger clients or partners they work with. If your business invoices other companies, you're part of a trust chain that can make you an intermediate target, not just a final one.

4. The warning signs that still hold up

Even though wording no longer gives the scammer away, certain behavioral patterns barely changed: the combination of urgency and secrecy, a request to change banking details only by email or chat with no phone confirmation, pressure to skip the usual payment-approval process, requests to pay in gift cards or cryptocurrency, and email domains that look almost identical to the real one but with one letter swapped. Any of these, combined with a request for money or sensitive data, is a reason to pause and verify before acting.

5. Concrete steps you can take this week

You don't need a security department to cut this risk significantly. A simple protocol — any unusual change to banking details or payment instructions always gets confirmed with a phone call to a number you already know — eliminates most "urgent manager" fraud. Turning on multi-factor authentication on corporate email and admin accounts (ideally with an app or a hardware key, not just SMS) closes one of the most commonly abused entry points. Properly configuring your domain's SPF, DKIM and DMARC records makes it harder for anyone to send email that spoofs your own address. And a short conversation with your team, walking through real examples of these schemes, usually prevents more incidents than any single piece of software.

What this means for your business

None of these measures require a major overhaul or a large investment — they start with clear protocols and simple habits any team can adopt.
