How to open an online store in Peru in 2026: step-by-step guide

Nov 18, 2026 · 6 min read · Raccoon Now
Raccoon Now · Blog

Opening an online store in Peru starts with registering with SUNAT, then choosing the payment methods Peruvian shoppers actually use at checkout, and only after that picking a platform and logistics. Peru has its own quirks — how much weight digital wallets carry at checkout, and how actively SUNAT now monitors sales made through social media and marketplaces — that are worth knowing before you launch. This guide walks through those steps in the order that actually makes sense to tackle them.

1. Register with SUNAT and get your RUC

To sell legally in Peru you need a RUC (Registro Único de Contribuyentes), issued free of charge by SUNAT. Most ecommerce ventures start as a "persona natural con negocio" (a sole proprietor), the simplest and fastest way to register; as revenue grows, many move to a legal entity such as an EIRL or an SRL, which separates business assets from personal ones. The process starts online through SUNAT's portal, though some steps still require a Clave SOL or in-person verification.

2. 18% IGV and electronic receipts: what SUNAT won't let slide

Peru's general sales tax, the IGV (Impuesto General a las Ventas), is 18% and applies to the vast majority of products and services sold online. Beyond the rate itself, you can't skip the electronic receipt: SUNAT requires you to issue an electronic boleta or factura for every sale, either through SUNAT's own free system, an authorized electronic service provider, or your own invoicing system. This isn't a minor detail in 2026: according to reports from April of this year, SUNAT is now actively monitoring sales made through social media and digital platforms, reportedly even using inspectors who pose as buyers to check whether sellers issue the corresponding receipt.

3. Payment methods: Yape and Plin dominate the Peruvian checkout

Peruvian shoppers expect to see their digital wallets at checkout. Yape (from BCP) and Plin (backed by a consortium that includes BBVA, Interbank, Scotiabank and other banks) started out for person-to-person transfers, but today they're a standard payment method in ecommerce too, each with millions of active users. To accept them without building each integration yourself, most stores use a gateway like Culqi, Izipay, Niubiz or Mercado Pago, which bundle cards, Yape, Plin and other methods into a single checkout. Compare fees and settlement times across providers before choosing one, since they vary by volume and business type.

4. Marketplace, your own store, or both

Mercado Libre is the highest-volume marketplace in Peru and, for a small business just starting out, the fastest way to validate a catalog without first investing in your own store. A store of your own (Shopify and WooCommerce are the most common platforms) gives you full control over brand, margin and customer data, in exchange for having to drive your own traffic. Brands that scale seriously tend to run both channels at once: marketplace for initial reach, own store for margin and long-term loyalty.

5. The complaints book: a legal requirement, not a design detail

Peru's Consumer Protection Code (Código de Protección y Defensa del Consumidor) requires every business serving consumers — online stores included — to keep a visible, accessible "libro de reclamaciones" (complaints book). On your site, that means a virtual complaints book linked from the footer or checkout. Skipping it isn't just a legal risk: for a Peruvian shopper still deciding whether to trust your store, seeing it there adds to the store's credibility.

6. Shipping: national couriers and urban last-mile delivery

For nationwide shipping, carriers like Olva Courier and Shalom cover much of the country's provinces, including areas other couriers don't reach. In Lima and other major cities, urban last-mile delivery services cut delivery times further; if you sell on Mercado Libre, Mercado Envíos simplifies that part by integrating directly with the marketplace. Decide upfront who covers the shipping cost, since it's one of the first things a Peruvian shopper checks before confirming a purchase.

What this means for your business

None of these steps is complicated on its own, but the order matters: sorting out your RUC and electronic invoicing before picking a platform saves you from redoing that work later, and adding Yape and Plin from day one keeps you from losing sales at checkout.
