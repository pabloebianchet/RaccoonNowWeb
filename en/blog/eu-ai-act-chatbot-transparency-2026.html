The EU AI Act now requires your chatbot to say it's AI: what changed in August 2026

Nov 11, 2026 · 5 min read · Raccoon Now
Raccoon Now · Blog

Since August 2, 2026, Article 50 of the EU AI Act has been in force — the rule requiring any chatbot or virtual assistant that interacts with people to clearly identify itself as AI. This isn't a proposal or something "coming soon": the obligation already applies, and if your business runs a support, sales or booking bot that reaches users in the EU, there's a good chance it applies to you right now, whether your office sits in Miami, Buenos Aires or Torino.

Who exactly does this apply to?

The AI Act works on a logic similar to GDPR: what matters isn't where your company is registered, it's who you're talking to. If you run a chatbot on your website, on WhatsApp or inside an app that converses with people located in the EU, the transparency obligation reaches you — whether you're a Miami-based small business, a Buenos Aires agency, or a Torino company serving clients in Spain or France. You don't need an EU subsidiary; it's enough that the service is directed at EU users.

What Article 50 actually requires

The core rule is simple to state, even if it isn't always simple to implement well: a person interacting with an AI system has to know, at first contact, that they're talking to a machine, unless that's already obvious from context (a bot clearly labeled "Virtual Assistant" with a visible bot icon might satisfy the rule with less text). In practice, that means your chatbot's first message — or a clear notice before the conversation starts — has to state that it's an AI assistant, not a human employee. A disclaimer buried in your terms and conditions does not count.

The nuance almost everyone misses: marking AI-generated content

The same Article 50 also requires marking synthetic images, audio or video that a business publishes — content that could be mistaken for real — as AI-generated, in a machine-readable way. There's a recent wrinkle here: through the package known as the "Digital Omnibus," the European Commission extended the deadline to implement that technical marking, until December 2, 2026, for systems that were already on the market before August 2026. It's worth not confusing the two obligations: identifying a chatbot as AI is already in force with no exceptions; the synthetic-content marking rule has that extra grace period only for pre-existing systems.

What happens if you don't comply

Penalties under the Regulation reach up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher, for most transparency violations. For SMEs and startups, the rule flips that logic: whichever figure is lower — the fixed amount or the percentage — applies instead of the higher one. That doesn't make the risk irrelevant, but it does make it closer to manageable than catastrophic, as long as the business can show it acted in good faith.

What to check this week

You don't need a months-long project to get compliant. The concrete steps: first, inventory every AI-powered touchpoint your business runs — website chatbot, WhatsApp bot, phone voice assistant, social media agent. Second, check whether each one discloses, from the first message, that it's an AI system, with visible text rather than just an ambiguous icon. Third, if your business generates AI images or video for marketing aimed at European audiences, review that separately from your chatbot — they're different obligations with different deadlines. Fourth, document internally what you reviewed and when, because that's exactly what a regulator will ask to see if a complaint comes in.

This article is general information, not a substitute for advice from a lawyer specialized in data protection and AI regulation for your specific situation.
