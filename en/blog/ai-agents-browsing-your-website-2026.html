AI agents that browse on their own: is your website ready?

Oct 7, 2026 · 6 min read · Raccoon Now
Raccoon Now · Blog

Until recently, "AI on your website" almost always meant a chatbot widget answering questions in the corner of the screen. That changed over the past few months: there are now AI agents that browse an entire website end to end — filling out forms, comparing prices across tabs, booking appointments — without a person clicking on anything. For a business, that's not a technical curiosity: a growing share of the traffic reaching your site is no longer a person browsing, it's an assistant acting on their behalf, and if your site isn't ready for that, you could be losing those visits without ever noticing.

Three announcements that show where this is heading

In January 2026, Google rolled out "Auto Browse" inside Chrome, powered by Gemini 3: a user describes a task — book an appointment, request quotes, fill out a long form — and the agent navigates, clicks and types on its own, without step-by-step supervision. Perplexity, for its part, made its Comet browser free (previously exclusive to $200/month subscribers) in late 2025, bringing autonomous browsing to a much wider audience. And in August 2026, OpenAI discontinued Atlas, the standalone browser it had launched barely ten months earlier, moving those same "agent" capabilities into a Chrome extension and the browser mode of the ChatGPT app instead. The takeaway is clear: regardless of which browser runs it, delegating web tasks to an AI is now a mainstream feature, and it's expanding fast.

What an agent actually does on your site

In practice, these agents show up to resolve specific tasks: comparing a product's price across several stores before deciding where to buy, filling out a contact or quote request form with data the user already gave it, checking an order's status, finding business hours, or booking an appointment. They aren't "looking" at your site the way a person would — they're reading its structure, locating buttons, form fields and text they can interpret precisely enough to complete the task without errors.

The technical checklist this requires

Whether an agent can actually use your site comes down to very concrete development choices. Semantic, accessible HTML: forms with correctly associated labels, buttons identifiable by text rather than an unlabeled icon alone, headings in a logical order. Critical content — pricing, stock, hours, contact details — that doesn't depend entirely on heavy client-side JavaScript without server-side rendering, because an agent that can't execute that JavaScript simply doesn't see that information. Structured data (schema.org) on products, prices and hours, so there's no ambiguity about what each piece of data means. And avoiding anti-bot measures so aggressive that they end up blocking a legitimate agent acting for a real customer with genuine purchase intent, not just a malicious scraper.

The security trade-off this opens up

The challenge isn't "allow everything" or "block everything" — it's telling the two apart. A firewall that cuts off any automated traffic protects against abusive scraping, but it also locks out a legitimate agent trying to complete a purchase or a form on behalf of a real customer. The better approach is finer-grained controls — per-session rate limits, intent checks instead of outright blocking, clear robots.txt rules about which agents can access what — rather than a single wall that treats all non-human traffic as a threat.

What this means for your business

This doesn't call for a full redesign overnight. It makes more sense to first audit the touchpoints that matter most — the contact form, checkout, the pricing page — and confirm an agent can read and complete them without tripping up.
