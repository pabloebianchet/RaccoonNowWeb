Shadow AI: what to do when your team is already using AI without your knowledge

Nov 4, 2026 · 6 min read · Raccoon Now
Raccoon Now · Blog

A Wakefield Research survey for PagerDuty, published in mid-2026, found that two out of three office workers admitted using at least one AI tool they themselves believed wasn't sanctioned by their employer. It's rarely malicious: people are just solving tasks faster with whatever is at hand. The problem is that extra productivity often travels together with customer data, contracts or source code that end up on a server the company never vetted or approved.

1. What "shadow AI" actually means

The term is the updated version of classic "shadow IT": someone installing an app without sign-off from the systems team. Shadow AI is the same thing, but with language models. Pasting a contract into the free tier of a chatbot to get a summary, copying a spreadsheet of customer records into an AI tool to draft a follow-up email, or installing a browser "AI" extension without checking what it does with the data it reads on every open tab. Nobody does this to hurt the company — they do it because it saves them half an hour.

2. Why this is more than an IT technicality

The risk has several layers. The first is losing control over data: on free tiers or personal accounts of many AI tools, whatever gets typed in can sit on servers outside the company's jurisdiction, and in some cases gets used to train future models. The second is the lack of an audit trail: if a customer reply, a contract clause or a price came out of an unauthorized AI tool, nobody can later trace how that conclusion was reached or fix the process that produced it. The third is regulatory exposure: a business handling customer personal data can end up with a data leak it doesn't find out about until it's too late to act.

3. Blocking access without an alternative doesn't work

The instinctive reaction for many leadership teams is to block access to generative AI tools on the office network. In practice, this rarely stops real usage — people keep using those tools from their personal phone or a personal account, except now the company has lost all visibility into what gets shared and with whom. An effective AI policy isn't about eliminating the use of these tools; it's about channeling that use toward secure options and giving the team an alternative that's just as fast.

4. A five-step AI usage policy

The first step is an honest inventory: ask each team what AI tools they're actually using in their day-to-day work, instead of assuming you already know. The second is a short list of approved tools on business-grade plans — the "Team" or "Enterprise" tiers of the main AI assistants, which don't train on conversations and come with admin controls — instead of relying on free personal accounts. The third is a simple, concrete rule on what information never gets pasted into an outside AI tool: customer data, passwords, unsigned contracts, proprietary source code. The fourth is naming someone responsible for the topic — it doesn't need to be a new role; it can be whoever already runs IT or operations, approving new tools and answering questions quickly. The fifth is short, practical training — twenty minutes with real examples of what to do and what not to do — which tends to prevent more incidents than any technical block.

What this means for your business

Shadow AI doesn't get solved by banning artificial intelligence outright; it gets solved by giving your team a secure way to use it that's just as fast as the unauthorized alternative.
